Hello everyone, today I'll be introducing our team's latest work—FineVLA, short for Fine-Grained Instruction Alignment for Steerable Vision-Language-Action Policies.

Simply put, the core idea of ​​this work is: we not only tell the robot "what to do," but also "how to do it." Not just what, but how.

So why do we need fine-grained instructions? Let's look at this comparison.

On the left are typical instructions from existing methods, such as "Pick up the cup."

These goal-level instructions are only one sentence, without specifying the direction of approach, which hand to use, which part of the object to contact, or the path the arm should follow.

The robot lacks execution-level guidance and cannot precisely control the execution method.

On the right are our proposed fine-grained instructions, such as "Use your right hand, approach from the left, and grasp the rim of the cup."

We annotate in 10 dimensions, including arm use, approach direction, contact area, movement path, and changes in object state.

This gives the robot controllable, fine-grained execution capabilities.

To achieve this goal, we built a complete open framework consisting of four pillars:

First, FineVLA-Tool and FineVLA-Data—a data construction toolchain and high-quality, fine-grained datasets.

Second, RoboFine-Bench—a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate fine-grained understanding capabilities.

Third, RoboFine-VLM—a scalable, deployable fine-grained video annotation model.

Fourth, FineVLA-Policy—a controllable execution policy trained using fine-grained data.

These four modules form a closed loop: first, build the data; then, evaluate the model's capabilities using the benchmark; use the evaluation results to optimize the annotation model; and finally, train the policy using high-quality labeled data.

Let's look at the data.

As you can see, four key figures are displayed in the upper right corner.

We integrated 10 publicly available robot datasets, covering a total of 972K operation trajectories.

Building upon this foundation, we used our self-developed FineVLA-Tool to perform manually verified fine-grained annotations on 47K trajectories, covering 10 annotation dimensions.

The average length of the annotated instructions increased from 9.3 words to 96.8 words, a 10.4-fold improvement.

The video below showcases our data browsing interface, allowing you to intuitively appreciate the richness of the fine-grained annotations.

Each trajectory is accompanied by a detailed multi-step action description.

Next, we introduce the evaluation system, RoboFine-Bench.

It comprises two tracks; let's look at the VQA track first.

We designed 1030 visual question-and-answer questions, covering 10 fine-grained dimensions, and evaluated them on 500 retained videos.

The evaluation is divided into two main categories: Grounding VQA examines whether the model can correctly identify specific details in the video; Reasoning VQA examines the model's reasoning ability regarding action execution logic.

The figure illustrates the question distribution across various dimensions and the specific design of the two evaluation types.

The second track is the Caption Track.

Unlike VQA, the Caption Track evaluates the model's ability to generate complete action descriptions.

We decomposed the Ground Truth annotations into 11,600 atomic facts and designed two scoring modes: Easy and Hard.

As shown in the statistical chart on the left, the Caption GT covers multiple dimensions, including action sequences, target objects, and initial configurations.

The flowchart on the right illustrates the complete evaluation process: first, the GT is decomposed into atomic facts; then, consistency is measured in Easy mode; and finally, coverage and illusion rate are further evaluated in Hard mode.

With this benchmark, we can objectively compare the performance of various models in fine-grained understanding.

Based on Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, we fine-tuned it on FineVLA-Data to train RoboFine-VLM.

The VQA results table on the left shows RoboFine-VLM leading significantly with a total score of 68.2, surpassing GPT-5.4's 60.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro's 59.6, and Seed 2.0 Pro's 58.5.

The Caption-Hard results on the right also demonstrate outstanding performance.

RoboFine-VLM ranks first with a total score of 82.2, achieving a consistency score of 80.4, coverage of 71.5%, and a hallucination rate of only 5.2%.

To more intuitively illustrate the differences in annotation quality, let's look at a specific case comparison.

The video above shows three perspectives of the same task—a zipper task: the robot needs to use both hands to zip up a backpack.

On the left is the output of RoboFine-VLM: accurately and concisely describing the entire process in just 3 steps.

Step 1: Grasp the zipper pull from the left with your left hand and lift it up, while your right hand approaches from the right.

Step 2: Pull the zipper horizontally to the right with your right hand to close the bag, while your left hand stabilizes the zipper strap.

Each step clearly defines the division of labor, direction of movement, and contact points between the arms.

Comparing this to the output of GPT-5.4 on the right, the same task is described in 5 steps, making the description significantly more verbose, and some steps lack crucial details about the coordination of both arms.

This example clearly illustrates the advantages of RoboFine-VLM in terms of accuracy and simplicity in fine-grained annotation.

Based on extensive simulations and real-world experiments, we have summarized three key findings.

This page introduces the first two.

First Finding: No Sacrifice—Fine-grained data does not sacrifice performance.

In all experimental settings, models trained using only fine-grained data outperformed models trained using only raw data, with improvements ranging from 1.4 to 8.1 percentage points.

A fascinating phenomenon is that the performance gap between OFT and GR00T, originally 6.4, narrowed to only 0.8 after using fine-grained data.

This indicates that fine-grained data possesses stronger cross-architecture generalization capabilities.

The second finding: Complementary – Fine-grained data and raw data are complementary.

As shown in the chart on the right, we observed a clear inverted U-shaped trend.

Performance peaks when the mixing ratio of fine-grained data and raw data is 1:1.

The optimal mixing setting achieves 86.8% in Easy mode, 82.5% in Hard mode, and 62.7% in the real environment, an improvement of nearly 13 points compared to 49.9% with pure raw data.

The third and most important finding: Steerable – Fine-grained supervision endows the policy with true manipulability.

A policy trained with fine-grained methods can produce drastically different execution behaviors based on changes in a single attribute of the instruction.

We validated our findings on five real-world robotic tasks, as shown in the GIF demonstration on the screen.

The first task, approach direction: specifying grasping from above and from the right, the robot indeed demonstrated different approach paths, improving the success rate from 60% to 78%, an improvement of 18 points.

The second task, arm selection: specifying left or right hand, the success rate improved from 60% to 64%.

The third task, object posture: recognizing upside-down and upright cups, the success rate significantly improved from 24% to 47%, an improvement of 23 points.

The fourth task, target color: distinguishing between blue and red pens, the success rate improved from 22% to 40%.

The fifth task, rotation direction: clockwise and counterclockwise rotation, the success rate improved from 76% to 86%.

These comparisons clearly demonstrate that fine-grained instructions give the robot true controllability.

The same task, described differently, can produce different execution behaviors.

Finally, to summarize our core message: Specify how, not just what.

It's not just about telling the robot what to do, but also about precisely describing how to do it.

FineVLA provides a completely open-source framework covering fine-grained data construction, benchmarking, and manipulable strategies.

The code, benchmarks, and models are all open-source; we welcome your use and feedback.
